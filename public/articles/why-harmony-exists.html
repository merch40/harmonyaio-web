Why Harmony Exists

Talk to your computer. It fixes itself.

BEAU MUNDT · FOUNDER, HARMONY AIO · 4 MIN READ
In this story
The nameWhat it actually doesThe small stuff counts tooWho it's forHow it got builtWhat's next

Every monitoring tool I have ever used is very good at one half of the job. It tells you something is wrong. Then it stops, and the rest is yours.

You still do the research. You still dig for root cause. You still decide whether tonight is the night you actually fix it or the night you put a bandaid on it and tell yourself you'll come back. Later never shows up. Anyone who has run a fleet knows exactly what that queue looks like a year in.

With a handful of servers you can stay ahead of it. A person can read the dashboard, triage it, and work the list. In a large environment you are staring at a wall of alerts, and the wall wins. Not because the people are bad at their jobs, but because there are more findings in a day than there are hours to chase them.

Harmony was built to close that gap. Ask it what's going on today. The goal is to bring the available findings together, work out what's wrong underneath them, propose a fix, and carry out the work the environment's permission policy allows.

The name

Harmony is named after my family.

Peyton is in college studying music, and plays double bass and bass guitar. He is where the musical idea behind Harmony starts. Weston is a firefighter, the one who runs in and takes care of the problem. Heidi is an RN, the one who makes sure the problem gets treated correctly and not just quickly. Brayden is the kid who doesn’t miss a thing, and makes sure the rest of us know about it.

I'm the orchestrator. Almost 30 years in IT, in environments of every size, and the guy who can see how the pieces go together.

That is not a metaphor I reverse engineered after the fact. The whole reason this project started was that I needed my time back for the people in it.

What it actually does

A Parent AI runs at the center. It brings together the context available for the environment, reasons over what the agents report, and proposes what to do next.

Child Agents run on the machines themselves. They are lightweight, they report what they see, and they carry out authorized work at the edge. Windows and Linux machines are the starting point. The wider ambition reaches across the devices people rely on.

Between the two sits the part that matters most. Every action passes a Yes / Ask / No permission model before anything runs. Read something? Yes, go. Restart a service on a production box? Ask. Some things are simply No, and no amount of confident reasoning gets past that gate. Every command is validated before it is dispatched, and every action is logged.

Built secure from line one. Every command is gated. Every action is logged.

Autonomy without a gate in front of it is not something I would put on a client network, and I'm not going to ask you to put it on yours.

The small stuff counts too

The wall of alerts is the headline problem, but it isn't where most people feel this day to day.

Their spreadsheet keeps crashing. A document won't print. A profile is corrupt again and nobody knows why. These are the tickets that eat a service desk alive. They are also the kind of work I want Harmony to take on, with the right context, permission, and a way to check that the fix actually worked.

Who it's for

Everyone I have been.

The person at home who wants to actually manage the devices on their own network. The family IT person that mom and dad call. The employee at a small business who handles IT along with fifty other jobs. The MSP running a fleet across a dozen client environments.

It is the same problem at every size. There is just more of it as you go up, and less help.

How it got built

Worth saying plainly, because it is part of why this is possible now and wasn't three years ago.

I have written a lot of code in my life. On Harmony I have written very little of it. I describe what I want, the model builds it, and I review what comes back. Going from writer to reviewer was a real adjustment, and the skill that carried it wasn't the years of code. It was the fine arts degree. Being able to hold a vision in your head and describe it clearly enough that someone else can build it turns out to be the whole job now.

We iterate. I explain what it should do, I read the implementation, and I can tell you what changed and why. That loop is how a platform this size got built by one person with a day job.
